## How Circuit writes a FortiGate

- **Circuit manages the sections the config describes**: the system's own settings, users, interfaces and tunnels, DNS, NTP, SNMP, logging, flow export, DHCP, routing, certificates, session helpers, and the firewall's addresses, services, policies, NAT and ACLs. Security profiles, VPNs, wireless and the rest of the device are left as they are. Inside a section it manages, an object the factory ships, such as the address `all`, the service `HTTP` or the certificate `Fortinet_SSL`, is kept.
- **A bridge puts the device in transparent mode.** A `bridge` interface joins VLAN interfaces, each its own VLAN, into one forward domain; the device then switches between them, filtered by the forward chain's rules, and routes nothing. Transparent mode needs a management address. One IPv4 address on a port or an interface outside every bridge becomes it, `manageip`, answering the management services on that interface alone, with static routes for its own traffic and no interface named in them. Without one, Circuit gives it `169.254.0.1/32`, on no network the device reaches, and the device is reached through its console. Every protocol is the service `circuit-all`, since transparent mode deletes the factory's. Leaving transparent mode is done by hand: a plan from transparent mode to a config without a bridge stops with a problem.
- **Otherwise ports are routed.** A VLAN interface sits on the one port or LAG that carries its VLAN in `trunk_vlans`. A port with `access_vlan`, or a VLAN carried on two ports, is an error: a FortiGate routes between its interfaces, and Circuit does not use its software switch.
- **A VRF is a number.** The first VRF in `vrfs` is 1, the next 2, and so on; the default table is 0. A static route goes in the VRF of the interface it leaves through.
- **A GRE tunnel's IPv4 address needs its peer**, `{ address, peer }`, because the platform keeps it as a host address with the far end next to it. A GRE or VXLAN tunnel leaves from the interface that has its `local` address, between IPv4 or IPv6 endpoints.
- **The management services answer on every interface with an address**, and so does ping. Who may reach them is decided by local-in policies ahead of the input chain: each service accepts `management.allow`, or its own `allow`, and refuses everyone else. The console is not affected.
- **A role is an access profile.** `admin` is the factory's `super_admin`. `operator` and `read-only` get profiles Circuit adds, `circuit-operator`, which changes everything but the system's own settings, and `circuit-read-only`.
- **The forward chain is the firewall's policies**, IPv4 and IPv6 separately, and the input chain its local-in policies. A rule that accepts replies, `state: ["established", "related"]`, needs nothing: a FortiGate lets replies through on its own. A forward chain whose default is not `drop` ends with a policy that accepts everything, since the device's own last rule is deny. Without `hardware_offload`, accepted traffic stays off the network processor, unless the rule asks for `offload`.
- **NAT is central NAT**, turned on when the config has any NAT rule. Source NAT is the central SNAT table, to the outgoing interface's address or to a pool of one address. Destination NAT is a virtual IP named `dnat-1`, `dnat-2` and so on, with the address it answers on.
- **A port's ACL is the platform's stateless ACL**, which only drops. Each rule that drops becomes one; what no rule drops passes, so rules that accept may only come after the last drop.
- **A policy becomes a route map for each family**, named after it for IPv4 and with `-v6` for IPv6, with the rules that can match routes of that family. A call to another policy is written out in place. A prefix set is a prefix list matching its prefixes and anything more specific; one that a registry fills has a single rule that refuses every prefix until `refresh` fills it, and a plan leaves its entries alone. A session without an import or export policy is given `circuit-reject-all`, which refuses everything.
- **A certificate with a key is the device's own; one without is only trusted.** Circuit compares a certificate by its text, and sends its key only when the certificate is new or with `--secrets`.
- **`management.ssh.weak_crypto` weakens HTTPS too.** The platform has one switch, `strong-crypto`, for the ciphers of SSH and of HTTPS alike.
- **SIP goes through the `sip` session helper.** The device is set to `default-voip-alg-mode kernel-helper-based`; by default its SIP proxy rewrites SIP whatever the helpers say.
- **Flow export samples ports, LAGs, VLANs and loopbacks.** A tunnel cannot have a sampler; its traffic is sampled on the interface it leaves through.
- **Local logs are kept in memory**, from `system.logging.local.level` up. Without it the device keeps none, and it never logs to its disk.
- **A change applies as it is sent.** Before the first line the device is set to reload its saved configuration after `--rollback` minutes unless the change is saved. If the device refuses a line, Circuit stops and reloads the saved configuration at once, which undoes what was sent. Otherwise it logs in again, saves, and sets the device to save changes on its own again; if that login fails, it reloads the saved configuration over the session still open. After a reload the device still reverts every change, so Circuit waits for it to come back and sets it to save changes on its own again.
- **Through a console, a fresh login on the same console proves access.** With `--console`, Circuit logs in on the serial console, whatever state the last session left it in: it quits a pager, leaves a `config` level, and logs out a session someone left open. After a change, it logs out and in again on the same console before it saves. That proves the logins still work, not the management path.
- **A management VDOM keeps the device's own traffic apart, and is not proved yet.** With one VDOM, FortiGuard, NTP, DNS and logging follow the routed traffic's default route, and get no answer when that path doesn't translate the device's own source. `vdoms.management` puts the interfaces it lists, and its own static routes, in a VDOM of their own, made the device's management VDOM; everything else stays in `root`. Its sections are read and sent there, the device's own settings under `config global`, and the management services are guarded there as in `root`. Until it has run on a device, `validate` refuses `vdoms`. Two steps are left to an operator, on the console, and a plan that needs either stops with a problem: turning multi-VDOM mode on (`config system global`, `set vdom-mode multi-vdom`, `end`), which moves everything into `root` and ends every session; and moving an interface into the management VDOM (`config global`, `config system interface`, `edit <port>`, `set vdom "<name>"`), once nothing in `root` names it. GRE and VXLAN tunnels and bridges are refused beside a management VDOM.
- **It runs without a licence.** A licence buys FortiGuard updates and support, and nothing here needs either. Everything Circuit writes was applied and read back on a FortiGate with no licence, over its console with no other cable plugged in. So Circuit's own SSH session was not tried there, no traffic went through the device, and no OSPF or BGP session came up.
